# write a script people want to finish

start with the part worth knowing. explain it in the order a listener needs, then stop when the opening promise is satisfied. give them something to learn; do not substitute hype for information.

## open with the reason to care

find a concrete change, inconvenience, surprise or unanswered question in the source. put that before the background. a useful hook tells the listener what they will get by staying.

**weak:** “Today we're looking at a new dependency caching feature.”

**better:** “Your lockfile matches. Your cache still might not.”

the first names a topic. the second gives the listener a mismatch to explain. the next sentence must explain what else has to match; the hook is not permission to omit the conditions.

aim to establish the subject within the first few seconds. cut greetings, “in this video,” and promises that something is “interesting.” if the opening could introduce almost any video, make it specific. do not invent a threat, personal experience or result to supply missing interest.

## make each sentence move the explanation

after the hook, answer the question it raises. then give the example, condition or consequence needed to understand the answer. order these for someone hearing the subject for the first time, not for someone reading the original report.

ask of each sentence: does it explain something, show an example, introduce a necessary limit, or answer a question? cut sentences that only announce importance or repeat the setup.

use the subject itself to make transitions. “A different lockfile won't match that entry” moves the explanation forward. “Now let's discuss another important consideration” postpones it.

an unanswered question can carry the listener into the next passage. answer it before switching subjects; do not withhold an obvious answer behind unrelated material. questions are one option, not a template for every paragraph. mix direct statements with short contrasts and longer explanatory sentences.

## write speech, not miniature headings

use words someone would say. name who does what. replace “subscription-to-organization binding” with “does this subscription belong to this organization?” explain an unfamiliar term before relying on it.

short sentences help when they clarify a distinction. a whole script of fragments sounds like a list of slogans. keep connected thoughts together, use contractions where natural, and leave room to breathe. humor belongs where it makes the explanation easier to follow; forced slang is another thing to decode.

## emphasize the distinction, not the scary noun

finish the wording before marking a few phrases for emphasis. choose words that change what the listener should conclude: “only on a cache hit,” “same organization,” or “not reproduced.” if nearly every sentence is marked, remove marks until the important distinctions stand out.

keep a qualification with its claim. marking “steal secrets” while burying “only if” elsewhere makes the claim less accurate. likewise, “no reproduced exploits” must stay together; a separate caption saying “exploits” can say the opposite of the sentence.

these are instructions for the eventual editor, not a subtitle implementation. short opening cues can give way to complete phrases once the explanation begins. colors, font sizes, animation and word timing belong to video production.

## end with what the listener came for

return to the question or benefit in the opening. state what the explanation establishes, or the next step it supports. “Try this on a repeat build” may follow a cache explanation. “Validate whether that old endpoint is reachable” may follow a conditional source concern.

stop when that promise is delivered. do not add a recap that repeats every point, a new unresolved topic, or an automatic request to like and follow.

## a complete rewrite

this example is invented to show the editing decisions, not to describe a real product.

**source:** “Builds reuse downloaded dependencies when the operating system and dependency lockfile match the cache entry. A miss downloads the dependencies. Compilation time has not been measured.”

**script:**

> Your lockfile matches. Your cache still might not.
>
> The operating system has to match too. Both are checked against a saved entry. No match? It downloads the dependencies again.
>
> A hit skips the download. It doesn't prove your whole build is faster; compilation time hasn't been measured.

the opening raises a question: why isn't a matching lockfile enough? the middle answers it and explains what happens on a miss. the ending limits the conclusion to what the source supports. emphasize “operating system has to match too” and “doesn't prove your whole build is faster”; leave the rest unmarked. “This cuts build time in half” would be a stronger promise with no supporting measurement, so reject it.

## where the approach comes from

[TikTok's Creative Codes](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en-US/creative-codes) recommends hook/body/close; its [creative guidance](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/creative-best-practices?lang=en) recommends introducing the proposition within three seconds. these are advertising recommendations, not a universal deadline or evidence that this script will retain viewers.

[Kapwing's caption guide](https://www.kapwing.com/resources/social-media-captions-styles-a-complete-guide/) describes selective emphasis and readable caption styles. it informs the editorial cues, not a claim that more highlighting improves engagement. measure audience response before claiming a retention gain.
